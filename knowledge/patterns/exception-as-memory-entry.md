# Pattern: Exception as Memory Entry

**Status:** Active  
**Origin:** LinkedIn No.19 (author voice)

## Pattern statement

Organizational memory often begins in **exceptions**—when the normal case stops working—not in standard procedures alone. What disappears fastest is the reasoning between the event and the decision.

## Core distinction

### Normal-case documentation

Processes, systems, and manuals describe what should happen and what was recorded.

### Exception memory

Who noticed, why the standard response was insufficient, what was checked, who was consulted, what was decided, and **why**—especially when a competent person deliberately deviates from the expected path.

Not every deviation is valuable (some are errors). **Intentional** overrides, escalations, delays, modifications, or rejections usually carry reasons worth preserving.

## Intuition reframing

"Expert intuition" is often **accumulated exposure to exceptions**: unusual condition combinations, failed approaches, near misses, local constraints, and prior decisions that worked.

When experts leave, organizations may say tacit knowledge was lost. More precisely: **memory of exceptions** was lost.

## Capture design (practical)

Do not record every action—volume without memory.

Focus on moments when someone **deliberately** deviates while context still exists. Not another report; a small question set, for example:

1. What was different this time?
2. Why wasn't the standard response appropriate?
3. What should the next person know?

Over time this builds a memory of **how the organization learns from reality**, not only a larger repository.

## Signals

Use this pattern when:

- Standard procedures are documented but repeat failures persist
- Incident reports exist but reasoning between event and decision is missing
- Experts are described as having "intuition" without extractable patterns
- AI/RAG over manuals does not reflect how operations actually respond under stress

## Core rule

> Standard processes tell us how work is designed. Exceptions preserve where that design met reality—and how people responded.
