# No.20 — Organizational Memory Doesn't Scale by Accumulation

## Source type

LinkedIn English version

## Original article

In the previous post, I wrote that organizational memory may begin with exceptions — moments when someone deliberately deviates from the expected path and leaves behind why.

But capture creates another problem.

The entries accumulate.

An operator stops equipment that technically could keep running. A project lead reuses an old workaround. Someone overrides a standard approval.

Each record makes sense on its own.

Months later, someone at another site faces a similar situation.

They find both cases. One team stopped the equipment. Another let it run. But why did the decisions differ? Do they still apply? What happened afterward?

The organization has more records.

It may not have more memory.

Memory requires something capture alone cannot provide: connection.

Connection across similar situations, earlier reasoning, and outcomes.

Without that, exception logs become another searchable archive.

Imagine the same equipment being stopped for the same reason at three sites, with none knowing the other cases exist. Or an exception returning every few months, with people reconstructing the reasoning each time. Or an exception recurring so often that it is no longer really exceptional — yet the standard never changes.

The information exists.

What is missing is the relationship between the pieces.

This does not require a large knowledge-management program. A few habits may be enough.

When capturing an exception, check for similar cases.

When it is resolved, record what happened next. Did the workaround hold? Did the equipment operate safely? Did it create another problem?

And occasionally ask:

Should this still be an exception?

Frequency, consistent reasoning, and recurrence across sites are useful signals. In our equipment-stop example, all three were present: the same exception kept recurring, for the same reason, across three sites.

When those signals appear together, it may be time to examine not only how the exception is being used, but whether the standard itself still fits reality.

Some exceptions should remain exceptions. Others may be telling us something about the standard.

AI could help here.

Not by turning every exception into a new procedure, but by connecting related cases, surfacing patterns, and bringing earlier reasoning forward when a familiar situation appears again.

That is different from retrieval.

Retrieval asks:

What do we have about this?

Organizational memory should also ask:

Have we seen this before? What did we decide? Why? What happened next?

No.19 was about capturing moments when reality departs from the process.

No.20 is about what happens afterward.

Accumulation gives us a repository.

Connection may be what turns it into memory.
