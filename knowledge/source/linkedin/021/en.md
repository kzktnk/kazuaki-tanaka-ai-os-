# No.21 — Organizational Memory Matters Only If It Returns to the Moment of Decision

## Source type

LinkedIn English version

## Original article

In the last two posts, I wrote about capturing exceptions and connecting them over time.

But there is another problem.

Even well-connected memory can sit quietly in a system.

A technician encounters an unusual condition.

A project manager faces a decision that feels strangely familiar.

An operator sees something that does not justify stopping the equipment — but does not feel quite right either.

Somewhere in the organization, something similar has happened before.

The problem is that nobody thinks to search for it.

Or they search after the decision has already been made.

This is not just a retrieval problem.

It is a timing problem.

Organizational memory becomes useful when it returns at the moment someone is deciding what to do.

Imagine an operator sees an unusual combination of vibration and temperature.

Instead of searching through hundreds of incident reports, the system quietly says:

We have seen a similar combination three times before.

One resulted in continued operation with no issue.

One resulted in a shutdown six hours later.

In the third, an experienced operator stopped the equipment despite all values remaining within limits.

Here is what they looked at.

Here is why they made that call.

Here is what happened afterward.

The AI does not need to tell the operator what to do.

In many operational environments, perhaps it should not.

Its value may be simply bringing the organization's prior experience into the room before the decision is made.

That distinction matters.

A knowledge system waits for someone to ask.

Organizational memory should sometimes know when it is relevant.

Of course, this creates another challenge.

Surface too much, and people stop paying attention.

Surface weak similarities, and the system creates noise.

Present previous decisions as answers, and yesterday's judgment may become tomorrow's automation bias.

So the goal is not:

“AI knows what we did last time.”

It is:

“AI knows when something we learned before may be worth considering again.”

That requires more than retrieval.

It requires context.

What is happening now?

How similar is it to what happened before?

What was different?

What happened after the earlier decision?

And is the old reasoning still valid under today's conditions?

The human still owns the decision.

But they should not have to make it as if the organization has never seen the situation before.

No.19 was about capturing exceptions.

No.20 was about connecting them.

No.21 is about bringing that memory back to where it can actually matter:

the next decision.

Organizational memory is not valuable because the organization remembers.

It is valuable because, at the right moment, someone does not have to start from zero.
