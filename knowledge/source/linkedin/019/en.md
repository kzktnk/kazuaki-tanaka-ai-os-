# No.19 — Exceptions Are Where Organizational Memory Begins

## Source type

LinkedIn English version

## Original article

Most organizations document the normal case.

Processes describe what should happen. Systems record what happened. Manuals explain what people should do.

But much of what an organization actually knows becomes visible when the normal case stops working.

A plant detects a condition within limits, but an experienced operator decides not to continue.

A customer request falls outside the standard process, and someone knows which rule can be interpreted flexibly — and which cannot.

A project encounters a familiar problem, but the previous solution existed only in a conversation that was never recorded.

These are exceptions.

And they may contain some of the most valuable knowledge an organization has.

The interesting part is what happened next.

- Who noticed it?
- Why was the standard response insufficient?
- What information did they use?
- Who did they consult?
- What did they decide — and why?

In many organizations, very little of this survives.

The transaction remains in the system. The final decision may be recorded. Perhaps there is an incident report.

But the reasoning between the event and the decision often disappears.

That is where organizational memory becomes practical.

If we want AI to support real operations, thousands of standard procedures may not be enough. They describe how the organization expects work to happen.

Exceptions reveal how the organization responds when reality refuses to cooperate.

An experienced employee often appears to have "intuition." But intuition is frequently accumulated exposure to exceptions: unusual conditions, failed approaches, near misses, local constraints, and decisions that worked before.

The employee remembers patterns the formal process does not.

When that person leaves, we say the organization has lost tacit knowledge.

Perhaps we should be more precise.

It has lost its memory of exceptions.

This also changes what we should capture.

Recording every action would create enormous amounts of data. A better starting point may be when someone deviates from the expected path.

Not every deviation is valuable. Some are simply errors.

But when a competent person deliberately overrides, escalates, delays, modifies, or rejects the standard course of action, there is usually a reason worth understanding.

AI could make those moments easier to capture.

Not by asking people to write another report, but by recognizing an exception and asking a few questions while the context still exists:

What was different this time?
Why wasn't the standard response appropriate?
What should the next person know?

Over time, this could create something more useful than a larger knowledge repository: a memory of how the organization learns from reality.

Standard processes tell us how work is designed.

Exceptions show where that design met reality — and how people responded.

That may be where organizational memory really begins.
