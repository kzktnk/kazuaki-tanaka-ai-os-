# No.19 — Exceptions Are Where Organizational Memory Begins

## Source type

LinkedIn English version

## Original article

Most organizations document the normal case.

Processes describe what should happen.
Systems record what did happen.
Manuals explain what people are expected to do.

But much of what an organization actually knows becomes visible when the normal case stops working.

A plant detects a condition that is technically within limits, but an experienced operator decides not to continue.

A customer request falls outside the standard process, and someone knows which rule can be interpreted flexibly — and which one cannot.

A project encounters a problem that has happened before, but the previous team solved it through a conversation that was never recorded.

These are exceptions.

And they may contain some of the most valuable knowledge an organization has.

The interesting part is not simply that an exception occurred.

It is what happened next.

Who noticed it?

What made them think the standard response was insufficient?

What information did they look at?

Who did they consult?

What did they decide?

And, perhaps most importantly, why?

In many organizations, very little of this survives.

The transaction remains in the system. The final decision may be recorded. Perhaps there is an incident report.

But the reasoning between the event and the decision often disappears.

That is where I think the discussion about organizational memory becomes practical.

If we want AI to support real operations, giving it thousands of standard procedures may not be enough. Those procedures describe how the organization expects work to happen.

Exceptions reveal how the organization actually responds when reality refuses to cooperate.

That difference matters.

An experienced employee often appears to have "intuition." But intuition is frequently accumulated exposure to exceptions: unusual combinations of conditions, failed approaches, near misses, local constraints, and decisions that worked before.

The employee remembers patterns that the formal process does not.

When that person leaves, we tend to say that the organization has lost tacit knowledge.

Perhaps we should be more precise.

It has lost its memory of exceptions.

This also changes what we should capture.

Recording every action would create enormous amounts of data and probably very little useful memory. A better starting point may be the moments when someone deviates from the expected path.

Not every deviation is valuable. Some are simply errors.

But when a competent person deliberately overrides, escalates, delays, modifies, or rejects the standard course of action, there is usually a reason worth understanding.

AI could make those moments easier to capture.

Not by asking people to write another report.

But by recognizing that an exception has occurred and asking a small number of questions while the context still exists:

What was different this time?

Why wasn't the standard response appropriate?

What would you want the next person facing this situation to know?

Over time, that could create something far more useful than a larger knowledge repository.

It could create a memory of how the organization learns from reality.

Standard processes tell us how work is designed.

Exceptions preserve something different: where that design met reality, and how people responded.

That may be where organizational memory really begins.
