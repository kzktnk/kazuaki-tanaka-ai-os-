# No.22 — A Standard Should Be the Memory of What We Have Learned

## Source type

LinkedIn English version

## Original article

We often treat standards as separate from experience.

Experience happens in operations.

Standards sit in manuals, procedures, controls, and systems.

But perhaps that separation is part of the problem.

Consider the exception from the last few posts.

An operator stops equipment though measurements remain within limits.

The first time, it is an exception. The reasoning is captured.

Later, a similar case appears at another site. The cases are connected, and outcomes recorded.

Eventually, the same pattern appears again.

At some point, we have to ask:

Should the organization still depend on someone recognizing this as an exception?

Maybe yes. Some situations are too contextual to standardize.

But sometimes the answer is no.

If competent people repeatedly make the same deviation, for the same reason, with consistently better outcomes than following the standard, the organization may have learned something.

Yet that learning often remains outside the standard.

People know.

The process does not.

That is a strange form of organizational memory.

The organization has learned, but its formal operating model has not.

Perhaps standards should be viewed differently.

Not simply rules from the past, but a compressed form of what the organization has learned.

An exception is not automatically a failure to follow the standard.

Sometimes it is evidence about the standard.

One occurrence may mean little. Repetition with similar reasoning and outcomes means more. Patterns across sites mean more again.

Eventually, the evidence may justify changing a threshold, adding a decision point, revising an approval rule, or updating a procedure.

Here, organizational memory becomes organizational learning.

Exception → reasoning → outcome → pattern → standard.

AI could help make that loop visible.

Identify recurring deviations.

Compare the reasoning.

Track outcomes.

Show where reality repeatedly differs from the documented process.

But AI should not silently rewrite the standard.

Changing a standard is consequential. Someone needs to review the evidence, understand the trade-offs, own the change, and decide when it becomes authoritative.

AI should not replace that governance.

It should make the learning visible enough to govern.

As operations become more AI-enabled, this matters more.

If AI systems learn and change faster than the standards governing them, the gap between operational reality and formal governance may widen.

We cannot freeze operations.

Nor can we let standards become irrelevant.

We need a loop between the two.

No.19: capture the exception.

No.20: connect related experience.

No.21: return it to the next decision.

No.22: decide whether it should change the standard.

Perhaps that is what organizational memory is ultimately for.

Not just remembering what happened.

Helping the organization become different because it remembers.
