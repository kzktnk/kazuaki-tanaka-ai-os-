# No.17 — How Much Authority Should We Give AI?

## Source type

LinkedIn English version

## Original article

As AI becomes more capable, we often ask:

How autonomous should AI become?

But perhaps that is not quite the right question.

The more important question may be:

How much authority should we give AI?

Capability and authority are not the same thing.

An AI agent may be technically capable of analyzing a situation, recommending an action, and executing it.

But that does not automatically mean it should have the authority to do so.

Consider an operational environment.

An AI might reschedule routine maintenance within predefined constraints.

It might adjust resource allocation when conditions change.

But should the same AI be allowed to shut down critical equipment during an abnormal event?

The technology may be capable of all three.

The authority should probably be different.

This suggests that AI autonomy should not be treated as a simple switch between:

Human decision → AI decision

Instead, authority can be designed in levels:

Recommend → Prepare → Act within limits → Execute with approval → Execute autonomously

And those levels should depend not only on the AI's capability, but also on:

the consequence of the decision,
the reversibility of the action,
the uncertainty involved,
the legal or regulatory responsibility,
and who ultimately owns the risk.

This becomes especially important in energy, finance, defense, healthcare, and other mission-critical environments.

The goal should not necessarily be maximum autonomy.

It should be appropriate autonomy.

Because the real design question for Operational AI may not be:

"What can AI do?"

but:

"What should AI be authorized to do — under what conditions, and by whom?"

The future of enterprise AI may not be about maximizing autonomy.

It may be about designing authority.
