# No.25 — AI Should Compress Work, Not Experience

## Source type

LinkedIn English version (final candidate; not yet published)

## Original article

In No.24, I asked what happens to the next generation of experts when AI takes over the work that used to train them.

The answer cannot be to keep junior employees busy with tasks we no longer need.

Nor is it enough to have them review AI outputs. Reviewing well requires the judgment that reviewing alone does not fully build.

We need to redesign how experience is acquired.

Consider a junior consultant preparing a recommendation for an asset maintenance program.

Previously, they might spend days reading inspection records, reconciling inconsistent data, and building a first analysis.

Now AI may do much of that in an hour.

That is a real gain.

But those days produced more than an analysis.

They were where a junior discovered that two sites used the same term differently, or noticed that a clean-looking trend concealed missing records. Where a manager asked, "What would make this conclusion wrong?"

If those moments disappear, the junior may deliver better work without becoming better at the work.

So perhaps the assignment needs a different design.

Before any AI summary, have the junior work through a small sample of raw records.

Then let AI organize the rest and prepare a first view. Ask the junior what cannot yet be trusted, which assumptions matter, and what they would verify with the site teams.

Before showing the AI's recommendation, ask for their own provisional judgment. Afterward, compare the two. Where did they disagree? What evidence would resolve it?

And when the decision is made, return to it later. What actually happened?

The point is not to make every task slower, but to be deliberate about which parts of the work carry learning.

A useful distinction may be between effort and exposure.

We can remove hours spent copying data without removing exposure to messy evidence.

We can accelerate drafting without removing the experience of defending a recommendation.

Not every old experience deserves preserving. And AI may create new ones: testing scenarios, exploring cases a junior would never have found, getting faster feedback.

The question is whether the person is building judgment or only supervising a finished answer.

This changes how we should measure AI productivity. Beyond time saved and output quality, teams that depend on expertise should ask whether people are becoming capable of harder decisions over time.

That may mean managers spending some of the hours AI saves on coaching, challenge, and feedback. It can look inefficient in a quarterly report. It may be essential over five years.

No.23 and No.24 asked which decisions and experiences should remain human. No.25 is about designing work so that both survive AI adoption.

AI should compress the work that does not need our time.

It should not quietly remove the experiences that teach us what to do when the answer is not obvious.
